# Transporter Room 2 — Shift Log
**Ensign M. Ryder, Transporter Technician**
**Day 12, 0600–ongoing**

0614 — Level-3 diagnostic on buffer 2. Routine. Expected null result.

0618 — Diagnostic returned a viable humanoid pattern in buffer 2. Pattern integrity 99.97%. Confirmed three times. This is not possible. A cargo refresh subroutine has been running against the buffer since day 6 — logged as automated maintenance. It should not be capable of maintaining a humanoid pattern at this resolution.

0621 — Hailed Lt. Cmdr. Okafor. Reported findings. She is en route.

0647 — Rematerialisation authorised by Acting Captain Okafor. Pattern materialised on pad 3. Male. Starfleet uniform. Vitals stable.

0648 — Lt. Cmdr. William Lloyd. Operations. He asked about an away team. There was no away team.

0653 — Escorted Lt. Cmdr. Lloyd to sickbay per Captain's orders. He remembers a surface mission that never happened. He called me "exceptional." I almost walked into a door frame.

0710 — Returned to bridge per Captain's orders while Lloyd underwent neural scan in sickbay. Reported to Okafor.

0745 — Lloyd arrived on bridge. Cleared for duty by Dr. Vasari. Captain directed him to the XO chair. He sat down like he'd been sitting there for years.

0750–0830 — I don't know how to write this part as a shift log so I'm just going to write it.

He identified the resonance frequency as Earth's Schumann resonance inside of two minutes. He deduced the field has a hard boundary from a six-minute gap in the approach sensor data. He asked me to pull power logs and then walked me through an analysis that found a heartbeat in our lighting grid. Not metaphorically. A QRS complex. Seventeen of them in forty-eight hours, getting stronger and faster, converging on sixty beats per minute.

It started on day 6. Six hours before the men got sick.

It was running on Okafor's cardiac trace for the first two days. Then something read Lloyd's medical file through the EPS subsystem — no user, no terminal, a pathway that shouldn't exist — and switched to his. When he left the bridge it switched back to Okafor's. Then it switched back to his while he was three decks away.

I don't know what's doing this. Nobody does. But it's in our computer and it's learning what a human heart looks like and it's getting better at it.

Lt. Cmdr. Lloyd asked me to find where the convergence curve flattens. I did. Sixty point zero four beats per minute. Thirty-one hours.

He said: "If it flattens at sixty, that's a resting adult human heart rate, and something has been assembling a person for twelve days."

I am going to finish my shift and then I am going to go to my quarters and not think about that sentence for as long as I can manage, which based on current neurochemical conditions will be approximately four minutes.

Ryder out.
